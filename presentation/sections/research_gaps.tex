% --- Frame 1: The Four Research Gaps ---
\begin{frame}{Research Gaps}{Four Gaps Guide Our Analysis}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reconstruction $\neq$ Correct Naming}\\
    SAE sparsity and fidelity do not guarantee correct labels --- \textit{vocabulary coverage} and \textit{image--text geometry} constrain naming

    \item \textbf{Dependence on External Resources}\\
    Every stage from raw activations to a clinical claim relies on external tools (ontology, generative phrasing, LLM judges) --- biases can be mistaken for properties of the representation

    \item \textbf{Reports as Selective Evidence}\\
    A report is partial: unmentioned concepts may be visible, absent, or incorrectly named --- judges can confuse \textit{omission} with \textit{contradiction}

    \item \textbf{Independent Labels Omit Relationships}\\
    Assigning labels independently ignores anatomical and causal relationships between findings --- embedding similarity $\neq$ anatomical hierarchy
  \end{enumerate}

  \note{
    We identified four research gaps that guide our entire analysis.
    First, achieving good reconstruction does not mean the nearest text label is correct. The vocabulary itself and the geometry of the image-text embedding space constrain the naming process.
    Second, the pipeline depends on external resources at every stage: the ontology vocabulary, a generative model for phrase expansion, and one or more LLMs as judges. Biases or errors in any of these components can be mistaken for properties of the learned representation itself.
    Third, radiology reports are selective evidence. Radiologists prioritize acute findings and often omit normal anatomy. An automated judge can confuse this omission with contradiction, unfairly penalizing correct concepts.
    Fourth, assigning labels independently to each feature ignores the relationships between findings. Embedding similarity does not establish an anatomical hierarchy or a causal relation.
  }
\end{frame}

% --- Frame 2: Our Response to the Gaps ---
\begin{frame}{Research Gaps}{Our Response}
  These gaps point to the broader difficulty of \textbf{assessing interpretability in a scalable and reproducible way}. We address them by:

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical clustering} at multiple resolutions to examine whether semantic groups produce useful concept categories
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Recognizing that embedding similarity alone does not validate clinical groupings
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Human + multi-model audit} to specifically address \textbf{evaluator reliability}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item 3 human annotators + 3 commercial LLMs on 25 stratified pairs
      \item Surfaces the pipeline's dependence on the judge's quality
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Label permutation} across forward passes to mitigate position bias in the LLM judge
  \end{itemize}

  \note{
    Together, these four gaps point to a broader problem: assessing interpretability in a way that is both scalable and reproducible is fundamentally difficult.
    We address these gaps in three concrete ways.
    First, we apply hierarchical clustering at multiple resolutions to examine whether semantic groupings of concepts produce useful categories. However, we acknowledge that embedding similarity alone does not validate clinical groupings.
    Second, and most importantly, we run a human plus multi-model audit specifically targeting evaluator reliability. Three team members independently evaluated 25 concept-report pairs, and we compared their consensus against MedGemma and three commercial LLMs. This directly surfaces how much the pipeline's results depend on the judge's quality.
    Third, we mitigate position bias in the LLM judge by permuting the label-to-letter mapping across three forward passes and averaging the probabilities.
  }
\end{frame}
